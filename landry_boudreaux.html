Landry & Boudreaux

Family History of John Kenneth Landry

Vermilion Parish, Louisiana · France · Acadia · 1548–Present

Your Heritage at a Glance

This family history records the genealogical research into the ancestors of John Kenneth Landry, son of John Henry Landry and Lilies Boudreaux. Both family lines are among the oldest and most deeply rooted Acadian families in Vermilion Parish, Louisiana — spanning over 400 years and 11+ confirmed generations, from France in the early 1600s to the present day.

Both lines survived Le Grand Dérangement of 1755 — the British expulsion of the Acadians — and made their way to south Louisiana, where they rebuilt their lives on the prairies and bayous of Vermilion Parish.

Mother's Line — Boudreaux

Through your mother Lilies Boudreaux, you descend from Michel Boudrot (b. ~1600, La Rochelle, France) — the single ancestor from whom every Boudreaux/Boudreau in Louisiana descends. His family survived exile in Halifax before arriving in Louisiana in 1765 with the Beausoleil Broussard party. Going further back, Michel's wife Michelle Aucoin was the daughter of Barbé Minguet and Martin Aucoin II — and Martin's parents were Martin Aucoin I and Suzanne Barboteau, whose own parents Pierre Barbot Barboteau and Marie Réveillaud (b. ~1548, La Rochelle) are the earliest confirmed ancestors on this line, stretching the Boudreaux heritage to 15 documented generations.

Father's Line — Landry

Through your father John Henry Landry, you descend from René Landry l'aîné (b. ~1618, La Chaussée, France) — one of the founding families of Acadia. After exile in Maryland during Le Grand Dérangement, the family arrived in Louisiana around 1769.

Cousin Marriages & Family Connections

How the Acadian families of Vermilion Parish intertwined across generations

Cousin marriages were the norm rather than the exception in Cajun south Louisiana. The Acadian exile of 1755 scattered a population of roughly 10,000–14,000 people. Those who reached Louisiana settled in tight communities along the same bayous, attended the same Catholic churches, and grew up together. Marriage records from the era frequently include "dispensation for third degree of consanguinity" — because the Church had formal procedures for cousin marriages, they were so routine.

Genealogists call this "pedigree collapse" — the family tree folds back on itself rather than expanding infinitely. In Vermilion Parish, virtually every old Acadian family connects to every other within three or four generations.
